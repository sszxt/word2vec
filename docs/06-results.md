# Results

Raw numbers: `results/results.csv` / `results/results.json` (generated by
`scripts/run_experiments.py`). All models: 1 epoch, SGD with linearly decayed
learning rate (`docs/04-training.md`), gradient clipping at norm 150,
hierarchical softmax (`docs/03-hierarchical-softmax.md`), evaluated on the
17,827 in-vocabulary analogy questions (`docs/05-evaluation-task.md`).
Trained on an RTX 5070.

The central limitation of every result below: text8 is ~17M tokens. The
paper's smallest reported data point is 24M words (Table 2) and its main
results use 783M-6B words -- 47x to 360x more data than we have available at
all. Every number here should be read as "the beginning of the same curve
the paper measures," not as a like-for-like comparison to their headline
numbers.

## Experiment A: CBOW vs Skip-gram (paper's Table 3)

Fixed dim=100, full text8 (16.7M tokens), full vocabulary (71,290 words).

| Architecture | Semantic | Syntactic | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| CBOW | 3.7% | 4.5% | 4.2% |
| Skip-gram | 14.6% | 20.3% | 17.9% |

Paper's Table 3 (dim=640, 320M words):

| Architecture | Semantic | Syntactic |
|---|---|---|
| CBOW | 24% | 64% |
| Skip-gram | 55% | 59% |

The qualitative pattern reproduces cleanly: **Skip-gram beats CBOW by a wide
margin on semantic questions** in both their run and ours (paper: 55 vs 24;
ours: 14.6 vs 3.7). Where it diverges: the paper's CBOW is competitive with
Skip-gram on syntactic questions (64 vs 59 -- CBOW actually wins), while in
our run Skip-gram wins on syntactic too (20.3 vs 4.5). The likely explanation
is data volume: CBOW's context-averaging acts like implicit smoothing that
helps most when data is scarce (our regime), while Skip-gram treats every
context word as its own training example and needs more data per word to
reach the same per-word signal quality (paper's Table 5 shows Skip-gram
specifically benefiting from more data/epochs more than CBOW does). At 17M
tokens we may simply not have crossed the point where CBOW's syntactic edge
shows up.

## Experiment B: CBOW dimensionality x data amount (paper's Table 2)

Vocabulary restricted to the most frequent 30k words, matching the paper's
methodology for this specific experiment. Total accuracy shown (semantic +
syntactic combined); full breakdown in `results/results.csv`.

| dim | 2M tokens | 4M tokens | 8M tokens | 16.7M tokens (full) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 50 | 0.35% | 0.65% | 2.15% | 5.87% |
| 100 | 0.38% | 0.64% | 2.21% | 6.24% |
| 300 | 0.27% | 0.48% | 2.07% | 5.96% |

Paper's Table 2 (same methodology, same accuracy metric, `24M`-`783M`
tokens):

| dim | 24M | 49M | 98M | 196M | 391M | 783M |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 50 | 13.4 | 15.7 | 18.6 | 19.1 | 22.5 | 23.2 |
| 100 | 19.4 | 23.1 | 27.8 | 28.7 | 33.4 | 32.2 |
| 300 | 23.2 | 29.2 | 35.3 | 38.6 | 43.7 | 45.9 |

Two things reproduce correctly:

1. **More data monotonically improves accuracy at every dimensionality** --
   our 0.35% -> 0.65% -> 2.15% -> 5.87% (dim=50) is the same shape as the
   paper's 13.4 -> 15.7 -> 18.6 -> 19.1 -> 22.5 -> 23.2, just starting from
   (and staying at) a much lower absolute level since our entire data range
   sits below their smallest data point.
2. **Diminishing returns from data are visible even in our narrow range** --
   the jump from 8M to 16.7M tokens (2x the data) roughly triples accuracy at
   every dimensionality, a steeper improvement than doubling from 391M to
   783M gives the paper (roughly +2-5 points there) -- consistent with being
   on the steep early part of the same learning curve rather than the
   flattening tail the paper's larger runs reach.

Where it does *not* reproduce: the paper shows a clear, monotonic benefit
from higher dimensionality at fixed data (dim 300 beats dim 100 beats dim 50,
consistently, by several points each step). In our runs the three
dimensionalities are within noise of each other at every data size, and
dim=300 is not consistently the best. The paper's own explanation for why
data and dimensionality must scale together (Section 4.2: "increasing amount
of training data twice results in about the same increase of computational
complexity as increasing vector size twice") implies the reverse also holds
-- extra dimensions need enough data to be worth fitting, and at 2-16.7M
tokens there may simply not be enough signal for a 300-dimensional model to
out-learn a 50-dimensional one within a single epoch.

## Experiment C: Skip-gram dimensionality sweep (full data)

Full text8, full vocabulary, extending Experiment A across dimensionalities.

| dim | Semantic | Syntactic | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| 50 | 10.4% | 14.4% | 12.7% |
| 100 | 14.6% | 20.3% | 17.9% |
| 300 | 15.4% | 21.9% | 19.2% |

Unlike CBOW (Experiment B), Skip-gram's accuracy *does* increase
monotonically with dimensionality here, at every step, on both semantic and
syntactic questions -- consistent with the paper's Table 6 (Skip-gram at
dim=1000/6B words reaches 65.6% total, its best configuration by
dimensionality among all their runs). The gain from 50 to 300 dimensions is
large (12.7% -> 19.2%) but clearly flattening between 100 and 300 (17.9% ->
19.2%, versus 12.7% -> 17.9% from 50 to 100) -- the same diminishing-returns
shape the paper describes in Section 4.2, just measured on a a much smaller
corpus.

## Training time

All figures from the runs above, single RTX 5070, batch_size=2048:

| Config | Steps | Wall time |
|---|---|---|
| CBOW, dim=100, full data | 8,164 | 17.8s |
| Skip-gram, dim=100, full data | 89,794 | 171.4s (2.9 min) |
| Skip-gram, dim=300, full data | 89,794 | 341.1s (5.7 min) |

Skip-gram costs roughly 11x CBOW's wall-clock at the same dimensionality,
directly reflecting the `C * (D + D*log2(V))` vs `N*D + D*log2(V)` complexity
difference from Section 3 (docs/02-architectures.md) -- Skip-gram does one
hierarchical-softmax prediction per context word in the window (up to
`2*C=20` per training position here), CBOW does exactly one prediction per
position regardless of window size. The paper reports a similar ~3x
CBOW-to-Skip-gram ratio on their hardware (Table 5); ours is larger, most
likely because our Skip-gram window (`C=10`) is wider relative to CBOW's
context (`N=8` total) than whatever the paper used for that specific timing
comparison.
