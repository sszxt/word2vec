# Results

Raw numbers: `results/results.csv` (one row per run) and
`results/results_summary.csv` (one row per configuration, with spread).
Generated by `scripts/run_experiments.py`.

All models: SGD with linearly decayed learning rate (`docs/04-training.md`),
batch-size-scaled gradient clipping (`docs/04`), hierarchical softmax
(`docs/03-hierarchical-softmax.md`), evaluated on the 17,827 in-vocabulary
analogy questions (`docs/05-evaluation-task.md`). CBOW uses the reference
`word2vec.c` context-gradient rule (`docs/04`). Trained on an RTX 5070.

**Every configuration is run over 3 seeds and reported as mean ± stdev.**
Earlier versions of this document reported single-seed numbers and then made
claims like "these are within noise of each other" — a statement that could
not be checked, because the noise had never been measured. It is about
**±0.2pp** on total accuracy. Several conclusions below depend on that number.

The central limitation of everything here: text8 is ~17M tokens. The paper's
smallest reported data point is 24M words, and its main results use 783M-6B —
47x to 360x more than we have available at all. Read these as the beginning of
the same curves the paper measures, not as like-for-like comparisons.

## Experiment A: CBOW vs Skip-gram (paper's Table 3)

Fixed dim=100, full text8 (16.7M tokens), full vocabulary (71,290 words),
1 epoch.

| Architecture | Semantic | Syntactic | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| CBOW | 6.03 ± 0.03 | 8.53 ± 0.44 | 7.49 ± 0.27 |
| Skip-gram | 15.57 ± 0.92 | 20.08 ± 0.19 | 18.21 ± 0.32 |

Paper's Table 3 (dim=640, 320M words):

| Architecture | Semantic | Syntactic |
|---|---|---|
| CBOW | 24% | 64% |
| Skip-gram | 55% | 59% |

The qualitative pattern reproduces: **Skip-gram beats CBOW by a wide margin on
semantic questions** in both their run and ours (paper: 55 vs 24; ours: 15.6 vs
6.0). Where it diverges: the paper's CBOW is competitive with Skip-gram on
syntactic questions and actually wins (64 vs 59), while our Skip-gram wins on
syntactic too (20.1 vs 8.5).

The likely explanation is still data volume — the paper's Table 5 shows
Skip-gram benefiting from more data/epochs more than CBOW does, and at 17M
tokens we may not have crossed the point where CBOW's syntactic edge appears.
But note that this gap narrowed substantially once CBOW's context-gradient rule
was corrected (`docs/04`); CBOW previously scored 4.2% here. It is worth being
suspicious that the remaining gap is also implementation detail rather than
scale.

## Experiment B: CBOW dimensionality x data amount (paper's Table 2)

Vocabulary restricted to the 30k most frequent words, matching the paper's
methodology for this experiment. Total accuracy; full breakdown in the CSVs.

| dim | 2M | 4M | 8M | 16.7M (full) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 50 | 0.32 ± .06 | 0.98 ± .14 | 3.67 ± .17 | 8.05 ± .18 |
| 100 | 0.30 ± .08 | 0.94 ± .09 | 4.09 ± .20 | 10.14 ± .43 |
| 300 | 0.25 ± .04 | 0.88 ± .13 | 3.63 ± .24 | 10.35 ± .55 |

Paper's Table 2 (same methodology, 24M-783M tokens):

| dim | 24M | 49M | 98M | 196M | 391M | 783M |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 50 | 13.4 | 15.7 | 18.6 | 19.1 | 22.5 | 23.2 |
| 100 | 19.4 | 23.1 | 27.8 | 28.7 | 33.4 | 32.2 |
| 300 | 23.2 | 29.2 | 35.3 | 38.6 | 43.7 | 45.9 |

**More data monotonically improves accuracy at every dimensionality**, matching
the paper — and our jumps are much steeper (roughly 2.5x per doubling near the
top of our range, versus +2-5 points per doubling at the top of theirs),
consistent with sitting on the steep early part of the same curve rather than
its flattening tail.

**Dimensionality is more interesting, and this is where a previous version of
this document was wrong.** It reported that the three dimensionalities were
indistinguishable and that this contradicted the paper. With CBOW's gradient
rule corrected and error bars measured, the picture separates into two regimes:

- **At full data, dimensionality helps.** 8.05 → 10.14 from dim 50 to 100 is
  +2.1pp against a ±0.2-0.4 spread — unambiguous, and in the paper's direction.
- **At 2M and 4M tokens, dimensionality actively hurts**, monotonically the
  wrong way (0.32 → 0.30 → 0.25 and 0.98 → 0.94 → 0.88).
- **dim 100 → 300 is flat even at full data** (10.14 vs 10.35, overlapping
  error bars).

That is not a failure to reproduce so much as catching the paper's own
Section 4.2 argument from the other side: "increasing amount of training data
twice results in about the same increase of computational complexity as
increasing vector size twice" implies extra dimensions need enough data to be
worth fitting. Below ~8M tokens we are demonstrably on the wrong side of that
trade; by 16.7M tokens the dim 50 → 100 step has become worth paying for, while
dim 300 has not yet. The paper, starting at 24M, only ever observes the regime
where more dimensions help.

The error bars are what make this sayable. The 8M row (3.67 / 4.09 / 3.63)
looks like a dim=100 win until ±0.2 reveals it as noise; the full-data row
looks similar in shape but is not noise at all.

## Experiment C: Skip-gram dimensionality sweep (full data)

| dim | Semantic | Syntactic | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| 50 | 10.9 ± 0.5 | 14.6 ± 0.3 | 13.05 ± 0.35 |
| 100 | 15.6 ± 0.9 | 20.1 ± 0.2 | 18.21 ± 0.32 |
| 300 | 16.0 ± 0.5 | 21.5 ± 0.6 | 19.20 ± 0.25 |

Skip-gram's accuracy increases monotonically with dimensionality at every step,
on both question types, consistent with the paper's Table 6. The gain is large
from 50 to 100 (+5.2pp) and clearly flattening from 100 to 300 (+1.0pp) — the
diminishing-returns shape of Section 4.2.

Note the contrast with Experiment B: Skip-gram keeps benefiting from dim 300
where CBOW does not. Skip-gram extracts more gradient signal per token (one
hierarchical-softmax prediction per context word, versus one per position for
CBOW), so at the same corpus size it is effectively further along the data axis
— and therefore further into the regime where extra dimensions pay.

## Experiment D: epochs x data amount (paper's Table 5)

CBOW dim=100, 30k vocabulary. Total accuracy.

| | 1 epoch | 2 epochs | 3 epochs |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2M | 0.30 ± .08 | 0.76 ± .10 | 1.43 ± .12 |
| 4M | 0.94 ± .09 | 2.94 ± .17 | 4.67 ± .05 |
| 8M | 4.09 ± .20 | 8.56 ± .04 | 11.40 ± .25 |

The paper states (Section 4.4) that "training a model on twice as much data
using one epoch gives comparable or better results than iterating over the same
data for three epochs, and provides additional small speedup."

**This does not reproduce here.** Three epochs beats twice the data at every
scale we can test:

| 3 epochs on X | 1 epoch on 2X | winner |
|---|---|---|
| 2M: 1.43 | 4M: 0.94 | 3 epochs |
| 4M: 4.67 | 8M: 4.09 | 3 epochs |
| 8M: 11.40 | 16.7M: 10.14 | 3 epochs |

The gaps are far outside the ±0.2 noise floor.

But that comparison is not equal-cost: three epochs over X tokens is 3X
token-passes, while one epoch over 2X is only 2X. Holding *token-passes* fixed
reverses the result:

| equal token-passes | more epochs | more data | winner |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4M passes | 2M x 2 = 0.76 | 4M x 1 = 0.94 | data |
| 8M passes | 4M x 2 = 2.94 | 8M x 1 = 4.09 | data |
| ~16M passes | 8M x 2 = 8.56 | 16.7M x 1 = 10.14 | data |

Both statements are true and not in conflict: **fresh data is worth more per
unit of compute than re-reading old data, but re-reading still helps enough to
be worth a 1.5x compute increase at this scale.** The paper's claim is that the
cheaper option (2X data, one epoch) matches or beats the pricier one (X data,
three epochs); we find the pricier option wins.

The most likely reason is scale. At 8M-17M tokens a single epoch is nowhere
near convergence, so additional passes still extract substantial signal. The
paper's runs start at 300M+ tokens, where one epoch already delivers most of
what the data contains and re-reading has little left to add. If that reading
is right, the disagreement is a prediction rather than a contradiction: their
claim should become true as the corpus grows, and testing it needs a corpus we
do not have.

Worth noting that 8M tokens x 3 epochs (11.40) slightly exceeds full data x 1
epoch (10.14) — one of the few places in this reproduction where a
compute-heavy configuration on less data beats simply using everything.

## Experiment E: frequent-word subsampling (NOT part of the reproduction)

Subsampling is from the NIPS 2013 follow-up paper, not the one reproduced here.
It is reported separately so it cannot contaminate anything above. Off by
default; `--sample`. Implementation and formula in `dataset.subsample_frequent`.

Threshold sweep, CBOW dim=100 full data (single seed):

| t | 1e-2 | 5e-3 | 3e-3 | 1e-3 | 1e-4 | 1e-5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| total | 8.2 | 10.2 | 10.9 | **11.8** | 9.8 | 1.7 |

The optimum sits at 1e-3, which is `word2vec.c`'s own default. The collapse at
1e-5 is a data-volume floor rather than a property of subsampling: it discards
two thirds of the corpus, and at one epoch what remains is not enough to train
on.

At t=1e-3, over 3 seeds:

| | without | with | delta |
|---|---|---|---|
| CBOW | 7.49 ± .27 | 11.59 ± .23 | **+4.1** |
| Skip-gram | 18.21 ± .32 | 19.87 ± .07 | +1.7 |

Subsampling helps CBOW roughly 2.5x more than Skip-gram, which follows from the
architectures. CBOW *averages* its context vectors, so a handful of very
frequent words dominate that average and removing them changes the represented
context substantially. Skip-gram already treats every context word as an
independent training example, so thinning frequent words mostly just removes
examples it was going to weight individually anyway.

Taken with `docs/04`'s fixes, CBOW has moved 4.2% → 7.5% → 11.6% without any
change to what the paper actually specifies — the first from a gradient rule
the paper leaves unstated, the second from a technique the paper does not
contain. The remaining distance to reference text8 implementations (~15-20%) is
plausibly the single-epoch limit measured in Experiment D.

## Training time

Single RTX 5070, batch_size=2048, mean over 3 seeds.

| Config | Steps | Wall time |
|---|---|---|
| CBOW, dim=100, full data | 8,164 | ~18s |
| Skip-gram, dim=100, full data | 89,822 | ~170s |
| Skip-gram, dim=300, full data | 89,822 | ~340s |

Skip-gram costs roughly 10x CBOW's wall-clock at the same dimensionality,
reflecting the `C * (D + D*log2(V))` vs `N*D + D*log2(V)` complexity difference
from Section 3 (`docs/02-architectures.md`): Skip-gram does one
hierarchical-softmax prediction per context word (up to `2*C=20` per position
here), CBOW exactly one per position regardless of window size. The paper
reports a ~3x ratio on their hardware (Table 5); ours is larger, most likely
because our Skip-gram window (`C=10`) is wider relative to CBOW's context
(`N=8` total) than whatever they used for that comparison.
